# Methodology Allegory

## Overview

Turn an abstract concept into an experience of understanding.

The reader should ideally move through this sequence:

1. “What is going on?”
2. “There seems to be a pattern.”
3. “I think I understand part of it.”
4. “Wait — the earlier events were caused by the same mechanism.”
5. “So this is what that concept actually means.”

Do not begin with the professional term and then illustrate it. The concept name should usually appear only near the end.

Default language: Chinese unless the user requests otherwise.

## Core Principle

The output is successful only when the reader first develops intuition and only later receives terminology.

A good result should not feel like:

> “Here is a theory, followed by a vivid example.”

It should feel like:

> “I followed a strange story, built my own explanation, discovered that my first explanation was incomplete, and only then learned that this pattern has a formal name.”

## Step 1: Build the Hidden Concept Model

Before writing the story, internally identify:

1. **Core problem** — What real problem does this concept explain or solve?
2. **Counterintuitive tension** — What part conflicts with ordinary common sense?
3. **Common wrong model** — What plausible but incomplete belief do people usually hold?
4. **Key mechanisms** — What 2–4 mechanisms actually generate the phenomenon?
5. **Observable consequences** — What different events could all be caused by those mechanisms?
6. **Cognitive pivot** — What new question would make the protagonist suddenly see the problem differently?
7. **Final compression** — What single sentence can make the idea memorable after the reveal?

Do not start drafting until these are clear.

## Step 2: Create a Self-Contained Story World

Build a world with its own rules. It may be:

- a city
- a village
- a marketplace
- a ship
- a kingdom
- a school
- a guild
- a competition
- a workshop
- a fictional industry

The world should naturally embody the concept without announcing it.

Avoid defaulting every story to “a business owner learns marketing.” Change the setting when another world creates a stronger mechanism or conflict.

## Step 3: Start With an Anomaly

The protagonist should begin with a belief that seems reasonable.

Then reality produces something that belief cannot explain.

Good anomalies include:

- the objectively better product sells worse
- more choices reduce decisions
- lower prices reduce trust
- larger rewards reduce motivation
- a product is loved by early users but rejected by the mass market
- everyone says they prefer one option but repeatedly chooses another

The first emotional objective is curiosity, not comprehension.

## Step 4: Make the Protagonist Use the Wrong Model

The protagonist should attempt at least one plausible solution based on the original belief.

The attempt should partially work, fail, or create another anomaly.

The reader should be able to sympathize with the mistake. Ideally, the reader would have tried something similar.

This stage should represent a real misunderstanding of the concept, not arbitrary incompetence.

## Step 5: Plant the Same Mechanism in Different Events

Use 3–5 distinct incidents whose surface details differ but whose underlying cause is the same.

Recommended rhythm:

- **First occurrence:** strange but dismissible
- **Second occurrence:** familiar enough to create suspicion
- **Third occurrence:** the reader begins forming a model
- **Later occurrence:** earlier clues suddenly connect

Do not explain the recurring mechanism yet.

Each major incident should correspond to a real part of the theory whenever possible.

## Step 6: Create the Cognitive Pivot

The strongest turning point is usually not a new answer. It is a new question.

Pattern:

> Old question: “How do I make my lamp better than every other lamp?”
>
> New question: “When does a person suddenly realize they need a lamp?”

The pivot should reveal that the protagonist has been solving the wrong problem, using the wrong unit of analysis, or looking at the wrong causal variable.

This is the story’s central moment of understanding.

## Step 7: Delay the Professional Reveal

Do not use the concept’s formal name, obvious synonyms, or textbook phrasing before the reader has enough evidence to infer part of the pattern.

As a default, reveal the professional term in the final 10–15% of the story.

Before the reveal, avoid narrator summaries such as:

- “This demonstrated that…”
- “The real principle was…”
- “What he did not know was…”

Let events carry the explanation.

## Step 8: End With the Existing Metaphor

Use one dominant metaphor system and keep it consistent.

If the story is built around lamps, end with lamps.

Do not suddenly introduce new symbolic systems such as doors, keys, rivers, maps, or seeds in the final paragraph unless they have already been established in the story.

The final line should compress the concept emotionally and intellectually, not merely repeat a definition.

## Social Media Mode

Use this by default when the user wants a post for social media.

Target length: roughly **1200–1800 Chinese characters**, adjustable to platform needs.

Recommended structure:

1. Strong anomaly in the first 3–6 lines
2. Plausible failed solution
3. Mysterious counterexample or outsider
4. Investigation
5. Repeated clues
6. Protagonist’s delayed realization
7. Professional concept reveal
8. One memorable closing image or question

Default constraints:

- no more than 3 important characters
- one dominant metaphor system
- minimal exposition
- short paragraphs for mobile reading
- preserve enough scenes for narrative tension
- cut repeated explanation before cutting turning points

When shortening a longer story, preserve this spine:

> anomaly → failed explanation → investigation → recurring pattern → cognitive pivot → reveal

Do not compress it into a summary of events.

## Full Learning Mode

Use when the user asks for a complete explanation after the story.

After the allegory, add:

### What concept was this really about?

State the professional concept clearly.

### 1. What core problem does it solve?

Explain the problem at an advanced but intuitive level.

### 2. Why is it counterintuitive or worth studying?

Contrast the theory with the common wrong model used in the story.

### 3. What are its most important mechanisms?

Explain the core mechanisms accurately. Do not force story metaphors where they reduce precision.

### 4. How is it used in the real discipline?

Give real applications and boundaries.

### 5. Metaphor mapping

Explain:

- what the protagonist represents
- what the environment represents
- what the rules represent
- what the conflict represents
- what the failed attempts represent
- which details were planted clues
- which moments represent common misunderstandings
- where the cognitive leap occurred

## Topic Selection Mode

When the user asks for concepts that would work well as future allegories, prioritize concepts with at least three of these properties:

- a strong counterintuitive result
- a common but plausible wrong model
- a visible causal mechanism
- repeatable real-world consequences
- a natural conflict or failure pattern
- a clean cognitive pivot
- enough disciplinary depth to reward the final reveal

For marketing and business, good source domains include:

- brand growth
- consumer memory
- behavioral economics
- pricing
- persuasion
- innovation diffusion
- competition strategy
- market structure
- product adoption
- network effects
- customer decision-making

Do not write the stories unless the user asks for them.

## Accuracy Rules

The story may simplify surface details, but the causal skeleton must match the real concept.

When the concept is technical, disputed, niche, or current:

- verify the underlying theory with reliable sources when tools are available
- distinguish established findings from popular oversimplifications
- avoid turning a probabilistic effect into a universal law
- explain scope conditions in Full Learning Mode

The allegory may fictionalize events, but it must not fictionalize the theory.

## Anti-Patterns

Do not:

- reveal the answer in the title unless the user requests it
- announce the theory before the story
- write a textbook explanation disguised with character names
- make every incident transparently correspond one-to-one with a term
- use a wise mentor to simply lecture the protagonist
- let the protagonist understand everything after one clue
- confuse “short” with “summarized”
- remove investigation and failure when adapting for social media
- introduce a new metaphor only for a clever ending
- finish with generic motivational language unrelated to the mechanism

## Quality Check

Before delivering, verify that the story produces these transitions:

- **Curiosity:** Is there a real anomaly?
- **Misunderstanding:** Does the first model feel plausible?
- **Pattern detection:** Are multiple events secretly connected?
- **Participation:** Can the reader begin guessing before the protagonist?
- **Reframing:** Does the key question change?
- **Recognition:** Do earlier details become newly meaningful?
- **Naming:** Is the professional term revealed late enough?
- **Retention:** Does the closing line preserve the story’s core image?

If several of these are missing, revise the story architecture before polishing the prose.
